Zechao Zhuo

I model megathrust fault slip in a 3D heterogeneous spherical Earth using a Bayesian inversion framework.

I bring multiple datasets into the inversion: continuous and campaign GNSS, seafloor GNSS-A, InSAR, DART, and recently SWOT.

I use postseismic data to constrain the earthquake rupture itself, by jointly inverting the rupture together with its physics-driven postseismic processes: rate-strengthening afterslip and viscoelastic relaxation.

About

An earthquake is not over when the shaking stops. For months afterward the fault keeps slipping, the mantle keeps flowing, and every GNSS antenna and radar scene in the region keeps recording. Much of earthquake science treats these postseismic signals as a separate problem, to be modeled only after the rupture itself has been settled. My work starts from a different premise: the earthquake and its aftermath are one physical system, linked by stress, and if we model them together, each one sharpens what we know about the other.

One of the central obstacles in earthquake source studies is non-uniqueness. Many slip models can fit the same observations equally well, and the differences between them are often supplied by modeling assumptions rather than demanded by data. I treat that ambiguity as a major object of study, not a nuisance to smooth away. I build Bayesian inversions in which the influence of every assumption is exposed and every dataset is asked what it can actually resolve. When the postseismic processes join the inversion, the problem becomes high-dimensional, time-dependent, and nonlinear; to accelerate the computation, I apply ensemble Kalman inversion and the No-U-Turn sampler, and am working on neural surrogate models.

Research

How this work began

My research began with a "can't fit." For my first project, I pulled a published coseismic slip model of the 2021 Mw 8.2 Chignik earthquake, computed the stress change it implied, and used that stress to drive afterslip — and found I could not explain the postseismic data satisfactorily. My advisor looked at the misfit and said: if you move the afterslip a bit closer to the trench, it will probably fit. But the way to move afterslip shallower is to move the down-dip edge of the coseismic slip shallower, and make its termination sharper. That is when I realized the two processes are coupled, and that I wanted to model them together.

In summer 2024 I spent three weeks with Dr. Junichi Fukuda at the Earthquake Research Institute, University of Tokyo, through the Sakura Science exchange program. There we decided to model afterslip together with coseismic slip, probing the fault slip, the heterogeneous fault frictional parameters, and the afterslip they generate simultaneously in a Bayesian framework using ensemble Kalman inversion — a method he was writing up at the time and has since published. That collaboration grew into the Simeonof joint inversion.

At SSA 2026 in Pasadena, I saw the first posters on the 2025 Mw 8.8 Kamchatka earthquake. The SWOT swath excited me, but I was puzzled about its real resolving power, and that of other datasets like DART. The pieces of an answer were already on my desk: my colleague Kai Wang had built beautiful Green's functions for this earthquake on a 3D spherical heterogeneous Earth, and I had taught myself GeoClaw in advance, out of suspicion about whether the GNSS-A observation at SEM1 near the Chignik rupture was actually coseismic, learning along the way that it can compute the dispersive wave problem. The Kamchatka project put all of it to work.

2025 · Mw 8.8 · Kamchatka Manuscript in preparation

Localized trench breaching in the 2025 Kamchatka earthquake

The 2025 Kamchatka earthquake is the first great megathrust rupture observed by the SWOT wide-swath altimeter, yet published source models disagree on whether slip breached the trench. We jointly invert GNSS, InSAR, DART, and SWOT data in a hierarchical Bayesian framework, on a finite-element fault geometry.

The joint posterior concentrates a peak slip of 22.8 ± 2.6 m at 12–13 km depth (158.95°E, 50.58°N) and yields a localized trench-breaching segment between 51.2°N and 52°N, with near-zero shallow slip elsewhere along the trench.

2020 · Mw 7.8 · Simeonof Manuscript in preparation

A stress-coupled joint inversion for the Simeonof earthquake

Afterslip is driven by the stresses the mainshock imposed, so postseismic observations should be used to constrain the rupture, not merely respond to it. We built a joint inversion that simultaneously solves for coseismic slip and its physics-driven afterslip in a Bayesian framework, applied to one year of data spanning tsunami waveforms, continuous and campaign GNSS, InSAR, and a seafloor GNSS-Acoustic site.

The joint model yields a more compact rupture than coseismic-only inversions and exposes two velocity-strengthening barriers of contrasting frictional strength — including the barrier that separated the Simeonof rupture from the 2021 Mw 8.2 Chignik earthquake next door.

2021 · Mw 8.2 · Chignik Published · JGR Solid Earth (2025)

Postseismic deformation constrains the coseismic slip of the Chignik earthquake

Published coseismic slip models for the Chignik earthquake fit the coseismic data equally well but differ considerably, especially in their down-dip extent, which places the stress-driven afterslip in different locations. We inverted a suite of coseismic models differing only in rupture extent, then tested how well each predicts the first three months of postseismic GNSS displacements through stress-driven afterslip modeling.

Only a compact rupture terminating abruptly ~130 km from the trench can generate the observed afterslip pattern, and matching the 3D displacements requires viscoelastic relaxation alongside afterslip — early postseismic data provide a genuine, independent constraint on coseismic slip.
